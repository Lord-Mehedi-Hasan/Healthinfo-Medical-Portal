Type 2 Diabetes

Also known as: Adult-onset diabetes, Non-insulin-dependent diabetes mellitus (NIDDM), Hyperglycemia

Medically reviewed by Dr. Sarah Johnson, MD
Board Certified in Endocrinology, Diabetes & Metabolism • Updated Oct 2026

Type 2 diabetes is an impairment in the way the body regulates and uses glucose (sugar) as a fuel source. This long-term (chronic) condition results in too much sugar circulating in the bloodstream. Eventually, high blood sugar levels can lead to disorders of the circulatory, nervous, and immune systems.

In type 2 diabetes, there are primarily two interrelated problems at work. Your pancreas does not produce enough insulin — a hormone that regulates the movement of sugar into your cells — and your cells respond poorly to insulin and take in less sugar (known as insulin resistance).

There is no cure for type 2 diabetes, but losing weight, eating well, and exercising can help you manage the disease. If diet and exercise aren't enough to control your blood sugar, you may also need diabetes medications or insulin therapy.

Signs and symptoms of type 2 diabetes often develop slowly over months or years. In fact, many individuals live with high blood glucose for years without noticing symptoms. Common symptoms include:

  • Increased thirst (polydipsia): Excess glucose buildup in your bloodstream pulls fluid from tissues, leaving you persistently thirsty.
  • Frequent urination (polyuria): Excess sugar in your urine draws water along with it, causing you to wake frequently during the night to urinate.
  • Increased hunger (polyphagia): Without enough insulin to move sugar into cells, muscles and organs become energy-depleted, triggering severe hunger.
  • Unintended weight loss: Even though you may be eating more than usual, the body breaks down muscle and fat stores for energy.
  • Fatigue and lethargy: Deprived of cellular glucose, the body cannot generate adequate cellular ATP, leaving you chronically exhausted.
  • Blurred vision: High blood sugar pulls fluid from the lenses of your eyes, impairing your ability to focus clearly.
  • Slow-healing sores or frequent infections: Impaired blood circulation and diminished immune phagocyte function slow recovery from cuts, blisters, and skin or urinary tract infections.
  • Numbness or tingling in the hands or feet (paresthesia): Prolonged high glucose damages tiny peripheral nerve fibers, causing peripheral diabetic neuropathy.
  • Areas of darkened skin (acanthosis nigricans): Velvety, dark patches of skin typically found in the armpits and around the neck, signaling severe insulin resistance.

When to See a Doctor

Schedule an appointment with your healthcare provider if you notice any persistent symptoms of diabetes or if you have risk factors such as being over age 35, carrying excess weight, or having a family history of diabetes.

Seek immediate emergency medical attention if you experience:

  • Extreme confusion, drowsiness, or loss of consciousness (signs of hyperosmolar hyperglycemic state)
  • Persistent nausea, vomiting, rapid deep breathing, or fruity-smelling breath (signs of diabetic ketoacidosis)
  • Blood sugar levels consistently above 300 mg/dL (16.7 mmol/L) accompanied by dehydration
  • Open, non-healing foot sores that show redness, warmth, or drainage

Type 2 diabetes is primarily the result of two interrelated physiological abnormalities:

  1. Insulin Resistance: Cells in muscle, fat, and the liver become resistant to insulin. Because these cells do not interact normally with insulin, they do not take in enough glucose.
  2. Impaired Insulin Secretion: The pancreas is unable to produce enough insulin to overcome this cellular resistance. Over time, pancreatic beta-cells experience metabolic stress and progressive secretory exhaustion.

Exactly why this happens is not fully understood, but being overweight, carrying fat in the abdomen (visceral adiposity), and physical inactivity are recognized as major contributing drivers. Adipose tissue releases pro-inflammatory cytokines and free fatty acids that directly impair intracellular insulin signaling cascades.

Factors that increase your risk of developing type 2 diabetes include:

  • Weight: Being overweight or obese (BMI ≥ 25, or ≥ 23 in Asian populations) is a primary risk factor.
  • Fat distribution: Storing fat primarily in the abdomen (waist circumference > 40 inches for men, > 35 inches for women) indicates higher visceral fat risk.
  • Inactivity: Physical activity helps control weight, utilizes glucose as fuel, and enhances cellular insulin sensitivity.
  • Family history: The risk increases substantially if a parent or sibling has type 2 diabetes.
  • Race and ethnicity: People of certain races and ethnicities — including Black, Hispanic, Asian American, and Indigenous individuals — are at higher risk.
  • Blood lipid levels: Low levels of high-density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol (< 35 mg/dL) and high levels of triglycerides (> 250 mg/dL).
  • Age: The risk increases with age, especially after 35, although type 2 diabetes is rising sharply in children, teens, and young adults.
  • Prediabetes: Having a blood sugar level that is higher than normal (A1C 5.7% to 6.4%) but not yet high enough to be classified as diabetes.
  • Pregnancy-related risks: Developing gestational diabetes during pregnancy or giving birth to a baby weighing more than 9 pounds (4 kilograms).
  • Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS): Having PCOS, characterized by irregular menstrual periods and excess androgen levels, markedly elevates insulin resistance.

Type 2 diabetes affects many major organ systems, including the heart, blood vessels, nerves, kidneys, and eyes. Managing your blood sugar and undergoing routine preventive screenings dramatically reduces the risk of serious or life-threatening complications:

  • Cardiovascular disease: Diabetes dramatically increases the risk of coronary artery disease with chest pain (angina), heart attack, stroke, and narrowing of arteries (atherosclerosis).
  • Nerve damage (diabetic neuropathy): High blood sugar injures the walls of the tiny capillaries that nourish peripheral nerves, causing numbness, tingling, burning, or loss of sensation in the limbs.
  • Kidney damage (diabetic nephropathy): The kidneys contain millions of delicate blood vessel clusters that filter waste. Diabetes can damage this delicate filtering system, leading to renal failure requiring dialysis or kidney transplant.
  • Eye damage (diabetic retinopathy): Diabetes damages the blood vessels of the retina, potentially leading to macular edema, cataracts, glaucoma, and irreversible blindness.
  • Foot complications: Nerve damage in the feet or poor blood flow increases the risk of serious foot infections. Left untreated, cuts and blisters can become severe ulcers requiring toe, foot, or leg amputation.
  • Skin and mouth conditions: Higher susceptibility to bacterial and fungal skin infections, as well as periodontitis and gum infections.
  • Hearing impairment: Hearing problems are twice as common in people who have diabetes.
  • Cognitive decline: Type 2 diabetes may increase the risk of Alzheimer's disease and vascular dementia.

Healthy lifestyle choices can prevent type 2 diabetes, even if you have biological relatives with the condition. If you've been diagnosed with prediabetes, proactive lifestyle interventions can slow or halt progression to full diabetes:

  • Eat healthy foods: Choose foods lower in calories, saturated fats, and refined carbohydrates. Focus on fiber-rich fruits, non-starchy vegetables, and 100% whole grains.
  • Get active: Aim for at least 150 minutes or more per week of moderate to vigorous aerobic activity, such as brisk walking, cycling, or swimming, plus 2–3 sessions of resistance training.
  • Lose excess weight: If you are overweight, losing just 7% to 10% of your body weight can reduce the risk of diabetes by nearly 60%.
  • Avoid long periods of inactivity: Break up long sedentary periods by standing, walking, or doing light calisthenics every 30 minutes.

Type 2 diabetes is diagnosed using blood tests that measure circulating plasma glucose and glycated hemoglobin. The primary diagnostic tests include:

Diagnostic Test Normal Range Prediabetes Diabetes
Glycated Hemoglobin (A1C) Below 5.7% 5.7% to 6.4% 6.5% or higher (on 2 tests)
Fasting Blood Sugar (FPG) Below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) 100 to 125 mg/dL (5.6–6.9 mmol/L) 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher
Oral Glucose Tolerance (OGTT) Below 140 mg/dL (7.8 mmol/L) 140 to 199 mg/dL (7.8–11.0 mmol/L) 200 mg/dL (11.1 mmol/L) or higher
Random Blood Sugar Test Below 140 mg/dL N/A 200 mg/dL or higher with symptoms

Note: Unless unmistakable hyperglycemic symptoms are present, diagnosis requires two abnormal test results from either the same blood sample or in two separate test samples.

Management of type 2 diabetes involves a multifaceted approach tailored to your personal A1C goals, cardiovascular health, and renal function.

8.1 Lifestyle Changes

Nutritional medical therapy with a registered dietitian, calibrated caloric intake, and regular aerobic plus resistance training form the cornerstone of every diabetes management plan.

8.2 Prescription Medications

If lifestyle modifications alone do not maintain your blood glucose within your target range, your doctor may prescribe one or more medications:

  • Metformin (Glucophage, Fortamet): Generally the first medication prescribed. It lowers hepatic glucose production and enhances insulin sensitivity.
  • GLP-1 Receptor Agonists (Semaglutide, Liraglutide, Tirzepatide): Injectable medications that stimulate insulin release when glucose is high, slow gastric emptying, and promote significant weight loss.
  • SGLT2 Inhibitors (Empagliflozin, Dapagliflozin): Oral medications that prevent kidneys from reabsorbing glucose, discharging sugar into urine, and providing cardioprotective and renal benefits.
  • DPP-4 Inhibitors (Sitagliptin, Linagliptin): Help reduce blood sugar levels with a very modest risk of hypoglycemia.
  • Sulfonylureas (Glipizide, Glimepiride): Stimulate pancreatic beta cells to secrete more insulin.

8.3 Insulin Therapy

Some individuals with type 2 diabetes require insulin therapy when their pancreas can no longer secrete sufficient natural insulin. Regimens range from once-daily basal long-acting insulin injections (glargine, detemir, degludec) to rapid-acting mealtime prandial insulin (lispro, aspart).

8.4 Blood Sugar Monitoring

Depending on your treatment plan, you may check and record your blood sugar using a fingerstick glucometer or wear a Continuous Glucose Monitor (CGM) that measures interstitial glucose levels 24/7 with customizable high and low alarms.

Proactive daily habits play a vital role in preventing diabetic complications and maintaining optimal energy:

  • Commit to managing your diabetes: Work with a Certified Diabetes Care and Education Specialist (CDCES) to formulate your self-management toolkit.
  • Inspect your feet daily: Wash your feet daily in lukewarm water, dry gently between toes, and check thoroughly for blisters, cuts, redness, or swelling.
  • Schedule regular dilated eye exams: An annual dilated retinal exam by an optometrist or ophthalmologist catches microvascular damage early.
  • Keep vaccinations up to date: High blood sugar weakens the immune system. Stay current on flu, COVID-19, pneumococcal pneumonia, and hepatitis B immunizations.
  • Practice proactive oral hygiene: Brush twice daily, floss every day, and schedule dental cleanings every 6 months to ward off periodontal infections.

Living with a chronic condition like type 2 diabetes can occasionally feel overwhelming — an experience known clinically as diabetes distress. Having a dedicated support team and mental health coping strategy is essential:

  • Join a diabetes support group: Sharing challenges with peers facing similar dietary and medication demands provides practical empathy and insights.
  • Consult a mental health counselor: Therapy can assist in navigating burnout, anxiety around glucose fluctuations, and depression.
  • Engage family and friends: Educate family members so they understand how to recognize hypoglycemia and support healthy meal planning.

Maximizing your consultation with your endocrinologist or primary care physician is easier with preparation:

Questions to Ask Your Doctor:

  • What is my current A1C target, and how often should I check it?
  • How often should I monitor my blood glucose at home?
  • What signs of hypoglycemia should I watch for, and how do I treat it?
  • Are my current medications protecting my cardiovascular and kidney health?
  • Do I need referrals to an ophthalmologist, podiatrist, or diabetes educator?

What Your Doctor May Ask:

  • What have your typical morning fasting and post-meal glucose readings been?
  • Have you experienced any episodes of shakiness, sweating, or lightheadedness?
  • Are you having any numbness, burning, or tingling in your fingers or toes?
  • What is your typical daily meal plan and physical exercise routine?
